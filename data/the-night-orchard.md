# The Night Orchard of Mirthapur

A novel in four chapters

---

## Chapter 1 — The House of Nine Limes

Anika Velthara lived at the House of Nine Limes, last house on Saffron Lane, in a town that maps politely refuse to print: Mirthapur. The limes were not planted in a square. They grew in a crooked nine, like a secret someone had started writing and then changed their mind.

Under the third lime tree — counting from the gate, never from the kitchen — Anika buried a moth-key. It was not a moth and not quite a key. It was a thin brass oval, no bigger than a thumb, with a moth stamped on one face and a notch on the other. She wrapped it in her aunt Meera's red handkerchief, the one with a single coffee stain shaped like a boat, and pressed the soil down with her heel.

She told nobody. Not Uncle Hari. Not the goat. Especially not the goat.

The goat's name was Pebble. Pebble wore a bell tuned to C-sharp, which made the neighbourhood dogs anxious and the postman oddly cheerful.

Every evening Anika walked the nine trees and counted them again, because in Mirthapur things had a habit of becoming eight when you needed nine. The moth-key stayed put. The handkerchief stayed red. The boat-shaped stain pointed, if you believed in such things, toward Glassreed Island.

---

## Chapter 2 — The Quiet Bell

Uncle Hari kept a shop called The Quiet Bell on the corner where Saffron Lane forgot to be a lane and became a rumour. He sold only umbrellas, and only umbrellas made in 1994 that had never been opened. He said an opened umbrella had already told its story, and he was not in the business of second-hand weather.

Hari knew about the moth-key. He had watched Anika from the upstairs window the night she buried it, and he had said nothing. He did, however, keep a ledger. On page seventeen, in pencil so faint it looked like dust, he wrote: *third lime, red cloth, boat stain, do not ask.*

The Festival of Unsent Letters was held on the seventeenth of the dry month. Nobody mailed anything. They stood in the square and read aloud the letters they had been too afraid to send, then folded them into the river. The river, which was named the Silt-Tongue, swallowed paper the way some towns swallow gossip.

Hari never read a letter. He stood under his unopened umbrellas and listened for cardamom on the wind. When he smelled it, he would tap the counter twice. That was the signal. The ferry would leave.

---

## Chapter 3 — Glassreed Island

The ferry to Glassreed Island was called the Cardamom Wren. Captain Biju Thorn would not start the engine for money, weather, or kings. He started it only when the wind over the Silt-Tongue smelled of cardamom — real cardamom, crushed, not the ghost of it in tea.

Anika boarded with mud still on her heel. She did not bring the moth-key. She brought the memory of where it slept.

Glassreed was not on any tourist map because the reeds there grew in sheets as clear as windowpanes. In the centre of the island stood the old post office, shut since the year of the uncounted limes. Under it, everyone said, was the monsoon vault.

Biju would not go inside. He sat on the ferry with a thermos and told Anika, "The vault does not want visitors. It wants a sentence."

She already knew the sentence. Aunt Meera had whispered it once, years ago, while staining the red handkerchief with coffee: *lilies forget the tide*. Not a poem. A password. Spoken to the brass moth on the vault door, and never sent.

---

## Chapter 4 — The Monsoon Vault

The door of the monsoon vault was the size of a cupboard and the colour of wet slate. In its centre sat a brass moth, wings slightly open, as if it had been interrupted mid-thought.

Anika did not have the moth-key with her. She had left it under the third lime tree on purpose. The key was for coming home. The sentence was for going in.

She leaned close to the brass moth and said, quietly: "Lilies forget the tide."

The moth's wings closed. Inside there was no gold and no monsoon. There was a single wooden shelf, and on it a stack of letters that had never been sent. The top letter was addressed to Anika Velthara, House of Nine Limes, Saffron Lane, Mirthapur. The handwriting was Aunt Meera's.

The letter said only this: *If you are reading this, the town still has nine trees. Put the moth-key back. Tell Hari the umbrellas may be opened once, on the seventeenth, and not before. Pebble's bell is C-sharp so you can find your way in the dark.*

Anika closed the vault. She took the ferry home when the wind smelled of cardamom again. She pressed the soil under the third lime tree to be sure. The handkerchief was still red. The boat-shaped stain still pointed at the island.

Pebble came down the lane to meet her, bell ringing C-sharp in the dusk. Anika scratched the goat behind the ear and went inside, leaving the nine limes to keep their count until morning.
